What a recipe is

A recipe is a film character and the darkroom decisions around it: where white sits, how hard the highlights climb, how much dye goes down, what texture the negative wears. One shutter press, developed two ways — drag the line.

Straight off the phoneToffee Neg

Find your film

One camera. Many ways to remember it.

Warm afternoons. Grey mornings. Harsh flash. Neon after midnight. Fonder films are built for moments, not test charts. Each one has its own colour, contrast, grain and character — so instead of fixing the photo afterward, you choose how you want the moment to feel before you press the shutter.

Film dials — Drift, Bleed, Compress

Three dials on the film itself: Drift leans the whole character further its own way or back toward neutral, Bleed lets neighbouring colours cross-talk like dye layers do, and Compress fades the deepest shadows the way a print lifts them.

The recipe

Ordinary Day 200

For nothing special.

Diary Neg with the colour turned up and the shadows opened — the recipe for days that do not announce themselves. It makes the day a little brighter and cleaner than it felt, without turning it into a style.

Best for — Everyday daylight — errands, tree-lined streets, the people you know. Not for moody scenes: it opens every shadow.

  • Colour — Greens and yellows come forward fresh and lively, reds stay a deep crimson, and a blue sky still lightens toward cyan. The whole frame sits a shade warmer, and skin keeps its own warmth.
  • Contrast — Gentle. The picture is a touch soft, with only a faint glow and the lightest darkening at the corners.
  • Grain — None. A clean frame.
  • Highlights — Left bright and clear — nothing is pulled down for mood.
  • Shadows — Opened, so the shade under trees reads as soft grey rather than black.

Try it when — The day should look a little brighter than it felt.

The recipe

Summer Chrome

For days you'll miss in December.

The strong-sun chrome, built on Daylight Cine: shadows cool while highlights warm, so the two ends of the frame disagree — and a face sits protected between them.

Best for — Open midday light with a real highlight in the frame — the headroom is built for it.

  • Colour — Golden light over faintly blue-green shade; only the ends of the picture are coloured. Skin is the one thing protected — a little richer, a little brighter, eased where the light lands.
  • Contrast — Carried by temperature as much as tone: warm against cool, with the sky keeping its blue instead of bleaching out.
  • Grain — None — the frame stays clean.
  • Highlights — A white wall or a windscreen rounds off gently at its brightest edge instead of going white.
  • Shadows — Cool, faintly blue-green, pulling away from the warm light.

Try it when — Strong sun, and you want to keep the heat in the picture.

The recipe

Night Ferry 800

For humid nights full of signs.

Humid neon — the warm kind of night. The glow is let run and the edge is taken off, so the air between you and the lights is part of the picture.

Best for — Dense signage after dark, ideally with water or wet ground to carry the glow. A dry, clean night gives it nothing to work with.

  • Colour — Red, magenta and cyan pushed hard while yellows are held back, so a sign separates from the street it is lighting. Faces barely move — ordinary between the signs.
  • Contrast — Low and close rather than sharp: a night that is warm, not crisp.
  • Grain — Coarse — 60, large. A fast-film night, worn openly.
  • Highlights — A bright source eases off early enough to keep its colour rather than burning to white.
  • Shadows — Lifted rather than crushed, deepened and turned blue.

Try it when — The air is thick and every shop is still lit.

The recipe

Midnight Club 100

For the last photo of the night.

Butter Neg aimed at a face lit hard from the camera in a small room at night — the snapshot from the end of a dinner, printed the way a corner photo lab once printed it.

Best for — People at close range after dark — a table, a bar, a friend lit by the camera's own light. It wants a face in the frame; a daylight landscape only turns yellow.

  • Colour — Everything light turns toward butter: walls, menus and a white shirt go cream, skin turns golden, reds on a sign keep their weight, and a dark jacket reads as navy.
  • Contrast — Soft in the middle, deep at the bottom — the corners darken the way a room falls away behind someone.
  • Grain — None. A touch soft and free of grain.
  • Highlights — The bright patch where the light lands on a cheek or a forehead is eased down rather than burnt white.
  • Shadows — Deep, with a cool edge — navy rather than brown.

Try it when — One more picture before everyone goes home.

The film

Toffee Neg

For photos that feel found, not taken.

Not a recipe — one of the films the recipes stand on, and the one this page's hero is developed through. It is also the roll on the homepage: loaded in Film Roll, shot blind, developed all at once.

Best for — Whole rolls. A look this settled suits shooting blind and meeting the pictures later, already warmed through.

  • Colour — A print left on a sunny shelf for years: amber soaked into the middle, leaves gone ochre and khaki, oranges and yellows glowing like toffee. The sky does not fade to grey — it turns a dusty teal against all the warmth, and skin stays golden without going orange.
  • Contrast — Soft and a little faded at the top of the frame, rich at the bottom.
  • Grain — A recipe decision, not the film's — load it clean or dial texture in.
  • Highlights — The brightest parts settle into a warm cream instead of white.
  • Shadows — Turned a deep chocolate brown.

Try it when — The day already feels like a memory.

How to choose a film

Start from the moment

  • Soft daylight, everyday errandsOrdinary Day 200 or Noon Neg
  • Bright sun and travelSummer Chrome
  • Warm low light and dinnersMidnight Club 100 or Late Supper 800
  • Neon, rain and city nightsNight Ferry 800 or Electric Rain 800

There is no correct choice. The same place photographed with a different film becomes a different memory.

Built before the shutter

Not another edit waiting for you

A Fonder film is more than a filter applied afterward. It decides how highlights roll off, how shadows sit and how colour behaves — in the viewfinder, before the shutter. You choose the film before taking the photo, so the visual decision becomes part of shooting, not another task waiting for you later. No preset hunting. No editing session. Just choose a film and use it.

Do not look for the perfect film

There probably is not one. Pick a film because it fits the day. Or because it does not. Use a warm film in cold weather. Use a night film at noon. The point is not to reproduce reality perfectly. It is to decide how you want to remember it.
