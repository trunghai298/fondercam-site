Building a film camera, frame by frame

Notes from building Fonder: films fitted to real camera renders, recipes that behave like the camera's own controls, and the app around them.

11 September 2026 – 28 September 2026 · 23 entries · Latest: A longer rewind, and 36-exposure rolls that develop, 28 September 2026

About these images

Every image on this page comes from my own camera files or my own phone, rendered through Fonder. Where a picture sits next to “the camera”, that side is the camera's own JPEG of the same shutter press.

Some photos used during development and local testing come from dpreview's public sample gallery. They're used only for testing on my own machines and aren't published here.

CC, CN, RA and NN stand for the camera simulations each fitted film follows. The film names are Fonder's own.

01Film engine and first looks

A film in Fonder is a set of colour moves baked into one lookup table, run through the same render graph as the viewfinder and the export.

11 September 2026

A film should change colour, not just fade it

The film colour engine went in on 11 September. Each film is a handful of colour moves (which hues turn, how quickly saturation rolls off, where the tones sit) baked into a 3D lookup table. The viewfinder, the capture and the export all run the same graph, so what you see while shooting is what you get.

The first palette mostly paled colours, which isn't what film does. Widening the chroma knee and re-authoring every film fixed that, and set a rule I still use: a film has to reinterpret hues, red toward orange and blue toward navy, not just take colour away.

16 September 2026

Black and white with real colour filters

Black-and-white films used to be a plain grey conversion. On 16 September they got a channel mixer, so each mono film behaves as if it were shot through a colour filter: a red filter darkens a blue sky, a green one lightens leaves.

02Halation, starburst and grain

The effects that make a frame read as film.

14 September 2026

Halation that real photos actually reach

Halation is the warm glow film gets around bright things, from light bouncing back through the emulsion. My first version only switched on above a threshold real photos almost never reached, so it did nothing. The fix on 14 September let it bloom at every dynamic range, at a level ordinary highlights reach. A later gain fix made it stronger, and on 23 September every look's halation was halved to match.

22 September 2026

A starburst for small lamps only

The starburst arrived on 22 September. It looks for small, very bright points, such as street lamps or string lights, and gives them soft rays. Large bright areas like signs and sky are left alone, which is what stops it looking like a lens filter.

24 September 2026

Grain the recipe owns, on a cleaner scale

Grain started as one global slider. On 15 September it became a grey, film-like grain matched against a reference camera's, and on 17 September each recipe got its own amount, so a recipe that promises grain carries it.

On 24 September the grain was rebuilt: finer and cleaner, with every recipe's grain moved onto the new scale. On that scale 30–40 is a light grain that reads as film, 60 is a coarse fast-film grain and 100 is the heaviest. Grain is sized for the resolution it's rendered at, so it looks the same on a phone screen and in a full-size file.

03Fitting the camera simulations

Four films were fitted to real camera renders instead of being authored by eye.

23 September 2026

How a film is fitted to the camera

Until 23 September every film was authored by eye. Then I started fitting films to the camera's own renders, from pairs of the same shutter press: the RAW, and the camera's picture of it with one simulation and every other control neutral.

  1. Collect pairs. One shutter press each: the RAW plus the simulation's rendering of it, at DR100 with tone, colour, colour chrome and grain off. Either the camera's own JPEG, or the maker's RAW converter changing only the simulation, checked against its settings sidecar.
  2. Develop the RAW the way Fonder does. Sharpening off and the camera's own white balance, so the film learns only Fonder's picture → the camera's picture.
  3. Align. The converter's lens correction magnifies the frame by 1.3–1.5% and bends it differently, so a tile aligner with a radial plus smooth-field model brings each pair to within 0.04–0.34 px. (It read vertical offsets upside down at first, which smeared the early proofs; fixed the same day.)
  4. Sample. Both images are averaged in linear light onto a 966×644 grid, one sample per 8×8 sensor pixels, and sorted by colour band and tone zone. Clipped areas, edges and thin cells are left out, and the tool reports which colours are still unmeasured.
  5. Fit. Bake a candidate film, render it through the real pipeline, compare with the camera in ΔE2000 and OKLab with every pair weighted equally, and adjust one number at a time until nothing improves. A chroma-tail term keeps the strongest colours from coming out pale.
  6. Check. Leave one pair out and score the film on it, compare against no film and every other film, render every RAW in full and look at it, and check that hues turn rather than fade.
  7. Name it, and set its first recipe from a recipe I actually shoot on the camera.

23 September 2026

Memoir Chrome, the first fitted film

Memoir Chrome was fitted on the chart's 47 patches (CC). A second chart, hand-held over grass in daylight, checked that the fit held away from the frame it was fitted on. Its first recipe, Still Air 100, came the same day.

24 September 2026

The fitted films on one street

The four fitted films, plus Olive Neg, on one frame at full strength, with every other control neutral. Olive Neg isn't fitted: it's a green-night film authored for the Underpass 400 recipe.

24 September 2026

Lost Summer Neg and Folio

Lost Summer Neg is the CN fit: fifteen pairs, twelve of them used for the fit. My first try only paled the reds, which broke the rule from the first week, so it was refitted until the colour came out strong and shifted.

Its first recipe, Folio, is set from a recipe I shoot on the camera: CN, Daylight with B−3, colour +2 and weak colour chrome. On the camera that recipe is DR400. In Fonder it opens at DR100, because Fonder's DR400 flattened this film where the camera's doesn't, and at DR100 it matched the camera's own frame of the same scene.

04Skin

24 September 2026

Protect skin, and a film-skin finish

Strong recipes shouldn't turn faces orange. Protect skin fades a recipe's colour on skin tones and keeps it everywhere else, and it deliberately leaves wood and other warm surfaces alone. The same day a film-skin finish went in for every film, and the portrait recipes were retuned for it. Portrait recipes open with no grain, because grain adds texture a face doesn't want.

There are no pictures in this chapter: the test frames are portraits, and this diary shows no faces.

05Recipes

Recipes sit on top of films: dynamic range, tone, colour, white balance, grain and the rest.

23 September 2026

Recipes set from the camera

Several recipes come from recipes I shoot on the camera, translated control by control: Still Air 100 and Ordinary Day 200 on 23 September; 1998 Neg, Shoebox 400 and Folio on 24 September; City Neg 400 on 25 September. Since white balance was rebuilt, each keeps its source's mode: an Auto recipe is AWB, a Daylight one uses the Daylight preset, and Shoebox keeps its fixed 6700 K. Halation and vignette are Fonder's own additions; the source recipes don't have them.

24 September 2026

Four films that were too alike

By 24 September there were too many films. A heatmap of the colour distance between every pair showed the near-duplicates, and four films were removed. Their recipes moved to the closest film left, and each move was checked side by side so the recipe kept its character. A shot or a shared recipe that names a removed one still opens, on its replacement.

06The intro

25 September 2026

A four-slide intro

On 25 September the one-line opening screen became four slides: film, not filters; recipes that behave like a camera; shoot RAW and change your mind later; and make it yours. The camera permission is asked for at the end, never earlier. The slide pictures are pre-rendered through the same render graph as everything else.

07Calibration and retune

25 September 2026

Controls calibrated against the camera

Until then the recipe controls were educated guesses. On 25 September each was measured against the camera, one at a time: colour, white-balance shift, highlight, shadow, dynamic range and colour chrome. For each one I converted the same RAW in the camera's own converter with only that control changed, and fitted Fonder's control to match.

Then every recipe was retuned onto the calibrated controls so it kept the look already approved. Most landed within 2 ΔE of their old look.

08White balance

26 September 2026

White balance, measured on the camera

White balance now works the way it does on the camera. A preset is an absolute light: Daylight under a warm bulb turns the picture orange, because the correction runs from the light the scene was actually lit by to the preset's light, not relative to auto white balance.

The presets were measured on my X-T3: one chart RAW converted at each preset and along a kelvin ladder from 3200 to 8300 K, then fitted through Fonder's own graph, with Daylight pinned to the camera's dial. Cloudy is derived, halfway between Daylight and Shade in mired.

Apple's kelvin and the camera's aren't on the same scale: the same RAW reads 5981 K in Core Image and 5026 K on the camera's dial, 31.8 mired apart, so every reading is moved onto the camera's scale first. The phone's live reading is used in the viewfinder, and a RAW's own as-shot light when it's imported.

The same day a seventh choice, K, made a fixed kelvin one tap away, so a camera recipe like “5200K, R+1 B−6” can be entered as written.

09Cine video

26 September 2026

Cine: video with the recipe baked in

Cine records 1080p at 30 fps with the recipe in every frame. Frames go through the same render graph as photos, then to HEVC with AAC sound. It records at the ratio on screen, cut before the render so no discarded pixel is ever graded.

At 30 fps a frame has 33 ms. The first phone take of the heaviest recipe, Nightshade 800, averaged 32.1 ms: right at the edge, with dropped camera frames and a stall on every recipe switch. What brought it down:

  • Grain from a bank. Three grain fields rendered once and dealt out with a shift each frame, so the grain still moves: 5.3 → 1.7 ms.
  • Halation glow at half resolution: 5.2 → 3.5 ms, with no visible difference (mean ΔE 0.001).
  • 4:2:0 straight to the encoder, and every camera buffer copied and released at once, which ended the out-of-buffer drops.
  • The recipe locked during a take and prepared between takes, so nothing is baked on the frame path.

After that the same recipe ran at 21.1 ms mean over 50 seconds with no dropped frames, and lighter recipes sit around 16–18 ms.

Heat came next. A hot phone pushed frames to 35–52 ms, and one frame took 822 ms: the first frame at a new render size was compiling its kernels. Now every rung of the render-scale ladder is warmed before the take. While the phone is cool the render steps 100 → 85 → 75%; at serious heat it can go on to 65% and 50%, with the glow at a quarter, and it climbs back only once the phone has cooled and the rung above will fit. The file stays 1080p throughout.

10Dynamic Range Auto

26 September 2026

Dynamic Range Auto

Dynamic range lets a recipe hold highlights by exposing lower and lifting the picture back, as the camera's DR200 and DR400 do. It now has Auto, which picks DR100 or DR200 (or DR400 when the ceiling allows) from how much of the scene is close to clipping.

The measure is the share of the frame whose brightest channel is at or above 90% of DR100's white, on a 256-pixel grid. It uses the brightest channel rather than brightness because a saturated blue sky clips in blue long before it looks bright.

ChoiceSteps up atSteps down below
DR2001.5%0.75%
DR400 (only with a 400 ceiling)18%, or 0.5% past DR200's white12%

It was calibrated on 35 frames: sun-and-shade and blown-sky scenes measure 2.6–35% and go to DR200, evenly lit scenes stay at DR100, and so does a colour chart. In the viewfinder it waits 0.5 s before stepping up and 1.5 s before stepping down, so it doesn't flicker. A RAW is measured from its own decode, and a Cine take locks its choice at the start.

11Viewfinder, preview and share

26 September 2026

A viewfinder without glass, and sharing with stats

The viewfinder lost its gradient scrims; every readout now carries its own shadow. The corner marks grow amber when a photo is taken, then settle. In Cine they stay amber for the whole take and a red edge runs round the frame; held sideways, a header with the film name and a REC pill runs along the top.

The photo preview prints the recipe and film on the picture, with five figures along the bottom: ISO, white balance, DR, highlight and shadow.

Share now offers three choices: Photo only, Card (the photo on a dark band with the recipe, the film and those five figures) and Overlay (the preview's label burned in). Card and Overlay drop location data before sharing.

12Film Roll

A third camera mode: load a film, shoot the roll without seeing it, then develop.

27 September 2026

Film Roll: load, shoot blind, develop

Film Roll is the third mode on the pill, next to Still and Cine. You pick a film (every film is available) and a roll of 12, 24 or 36 exposures. The roll stays locked to that film until it's developed. While shooting you see either negatives or no preview at all, and the last-shot tile only plays the film advancing.

When the roll is finished, you develop it. You choose a border (none, weathered or scanned) and, if you like, a date stamp from each frame's capture time. The stamp is drawn as amber seven-segment digits with a soft glow, the way old compact cameras printed it, in four formats such as '26 9 27 or 27 9 '26 14:32.

Every roll develops under one lab recipe, the same for every film, tuned for what a good lab scan would give:

  • Colour +3 with weak colour chrome and weak colour chrome blue (none of the three on black-and-white film)
  • Highlight −1, Shadow +0.5
  • Dynamic Range Auto up to DR200, decided per frame
  • AWB with no shifts, Clarity −1, Sharpness −1
  • A light, fine grain (25 on colour, 30 on black and white), halation 10 and vignette 10

Roll photos live only in their roll: they stay out of the Photos grid and its counts, and a Card or Overlay share prints the film name and frame number instead of settings. The same release added multi-select in Photos, to save, share or delete several photos at once.

27 September 2026

The end of a roll

In the last three frames of a roll (10–12, 22–24 or 34–36) the counter beside the shutter turns amber and pulses after each shot. There's a light click on entering the last three frames and a firmer one on the last. On the last frame the line under the number reads LAST FRAME, and the film advance runs slower, as if the film were getting tight. Nothing blocks shooting.

When the last frame is taken, the strip winds back into the film's own canister, the canister turns and settles, and Roll complete fades in with the Develop button. It plays once per roll, and a tap skips it.

28 September 2026

A longer rewind, and 36-exposure rolls that develop

The rewind now follows the length of the roll: 1.8 s for 12 exposures, 2.8 s for 24 and 3.6 s for 36, easing slow–fast–slow. Each frame wound back gives one light crank tick, with a small knock on the canister. The canister is bigger now and sits on a soft panel with a faint amber glow.

The more important fix was underneath. Developing a 24 or 36-exposure roll crashed the app after about 15 frames. Each frame renders twice (a preview, then full resolution) on serial queues that only freed their temporary buffers when they went idle, and during a roll they never do. About 150 MB per ProRAW frame piled up until iOS killed the app. Now both queues release memory after every frame.
